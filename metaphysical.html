Chapter 01 · Genesis

The beginning: wonder, evidence, and meaning

Genesis opens with a theological vision of creation. Science studies the universe through observation and testable explanations. Read these perspectives in conversation, without asking one to stand in for the other.

Read the text in context

Creation, goodness, and human responsibility

Begin with Genesis 1:1–5. Its opening movement names God as creator and portrays light, order, and goodness. Read the whole chapter and notice its repeated rhythm, imagery, and ancient literary setting; Christians and other readers interpret these passages in different ways.

Hebrews 11:3 reflects on creation through faith. This is a theological statement, not a scientific measurement or a claim that faith is a substitute for evidence. Genesis can invite readers to consider the worth of creation and the responsibility to care for life.

Reflect: What does this story ask you to value, protect, or treat with humility?

What evidence can tell us

A universe with a long, changing history

Current cosmology describes an observable universe that has expanded and cooled from an early, hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. The Big Bang model is supported by multiple observations; it does not establish what, if anything, preceded that state or prove that the universe began as a known physical singularity.

In the first minutes, nuclear processes formed mostly hydrogen and helium nuclei, with traces of other light elements. Much later, stars forged many heavier elements. The equation E = mc² describes the relationship between mass and rest energy; it does not show that matter is an illusion or prove a spiritual cause. Atoms are structured by quantum behavior and physical interactions, and quantum vacuum effects are not evidence that particles appear from absolute nothing.

Reflect: Which claims here are observations, which are models, and which questions remain open?

Further reading: NASA: The universe’s history.

Knowledge put into practice

Build with care for people and the planet

Technologies such as computer chips use tested knowledge of materials, electricity, and information. Their workings can inspire wonder, but engineering is not scientific proof of a spiritual blueprint. Metaphors can open questions; they should not be mistaken for measurements.

A useful question for every field is not only “Can we build it?” but “Who benefits, who may be harmed, and how can we make it accessible, accountable, and sustainable?” These questions connect learning with dignity and the common good.

Reflect: What challenge in your community or field could a thoughtful, responsible use of knowledge help address?

This chapter is a starting point, not a complete account of cosmology, biblical interpretation, or technology. Evidence and interpretations can develop.
